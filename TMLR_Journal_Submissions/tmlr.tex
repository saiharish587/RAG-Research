\begin{abstract}
Small Language Models (SLMs) containing fewer than 1 billion parameters possess restricted parametric memory capacity for multi-hop factual reasoning. While Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is widely applied to externalize memory, current RAG literature focuses almost exclusively on multi-billion parameter models (7B to 70B+ parameters). In this work, we present a comprehensive 60,000-run controlled empirical study evaluating Liquid AI's LFM2 architecture, the only sub-1B family tested, across 5 distinct RAG architectural arms (No-RAG, Oracle RAG, Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, and Modular RAG) on 600 questions from the HotpotQA Distractor development set; other sub-1B families are left for future work. Each configuration is evaluated across 10 repeated executions on an AMD Ryzen 7 6800H host equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Laptop GPU (4GB VRAM); under deterministic decoding the repetitions produce identical outputs and measure latency variability. Among the retrieval-based arms, single-stage Naive RAG achieves the highest Token F1 ($0.1107 \pm 0.2130$) and exact match rate ($0.0367 \pm 0.1880$) at the lowest mean inference latency ($0.581 \pm 0.209$ seconds) on LFM2-350M. Increasing RAG pipeline complexity in Advanced RAG yields comparable quality ($0.1017 \pm 0.1948$ Token F1) at 2.5 times higher latency ($1.460 \pm 0.301$ seconds), whereas Modular RAG suffers severe performance degradation ($0.0218 \pm 0.0628$ Token F1) because, under unconstrained prompt routing, the generator answered ``no'' to the retrieval decision and bypassed retrieval in 94\% (LFM2-350M) and 97\% (LFM2-700M) of runs. Within the LFM2 family, LFM2-700M has lower Naive RAG Token F1 than LFM2-350M ($0.0727 \pm 0.1055$ versus $0.1107$) and lower exact match, but higher token recall ($0.587$ versus $0.477$). The direction of this scale effect therefore depends on the metric: the larger model covers more gold tokens but with lower precision, and the Token F1 gap disappears or reverses when responses of similar length are compared. Under 100\% gold evidence context (Oracle RAG gold-context reference baseline), LFM2-350M achieves $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1, outperforming all retrieval-based arms, while LFM2-700M achieves $0.1163 \pm 0.1402$ Token F1. All code, vector database artifacts, benchmark results, and raw execution logs for 56,941 of the 60,000 runs are publicly available at \openreview.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large Language Models (LLMs) with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across factual question answering, reading comprehension, and logical reasoning tasks. However, deploying multi-billion parameter models requires high-memory server GPUs, introducing substantial monetary costs, energy footprint, and privacy risks associated with cloud API dependency. Consequently, Small Language Models (SLMs) containing fewer than 1 billion parameters are increasingly targeted for local, privacy-preserving, and edge deployment on resource-constrained hardware such as laptop GPUs and mobile processors.

Due to their compact parameter footprint, sub-billion parameter models possess limited capacity to memorize multi-hop factual knowledge in their static network weights \cite{mallen2023when, gao2023retrieval}. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020retrieval} addresses parametric memory bounds by dynamically fetching relevant external document passages from a corpus and appending them into the model's prompt context.

While sophisticated RAG architectural paradigms incorporating query rewriting \cite{ma2023query}, cross-encoder reranking \cite{nogueira2019passage}, and dynamic sub-query routing with reciprocal rank fusion \cite{cormack2009reciprocal} have been developed for multi-billion parameter LLMs \cite{li2024retrieval, asai2023self}, it remains fundamentally unclear whether these complex abstractions benefit sub-1B language models. Most existing RAG benchmarks evaluate models such as Llama-2, Mixtral-8x7B, or GPT-4 \cite{gao2023retrieval}, leaving a substantial empirical knowledge gap regarding RAG behavior in the sub-1B parameter regime.

To address this gap, we present a full factorial controlled empirical study comprising 60,000 executed benchmark runs across two parameter scales of Liquid AI's LFM2 architecture (LFM2-350M and LFM2-700M) \cite{liquid2024lfm} and five distinct RAG architectural arms on the HotpotQA Distractor benchmark \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. We restrict our conclusions to this family and benchmark; other sub-1B families, larger models, and other tasks are left for future work. All evaluations were conducted locally on a standardized hardware environment (AMD Ryzen 7 6800H CPU, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Laptop GPU with 4GB VRAM) under Windows 11.

Our key empirical findings and contributions are structured as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Best Observed Quality-Latency Trade-Off under Naive RAG}: Single-stage vector retrieval (Naive RAG \cite{lewis2020retrieval}) delivers the best observed accuracy-latency trade-off ($0.1107 \pm 0.2130$ Token F1, $0.0367 \pm 0.1880$ Exact Match \cite{rajpurkar2016squad}) at the lowest latency among retrieval-based arms ($0.581 \pm 0.209$s) on LFM2-350M \cite{liquid2024lfm}. Multi-stage Advanced RAG takes 2.5 times the latency ($1.460 \pm 0.301$s) without statistically significant quality gain at either scale ($\bar{d}=0.0089, p=0.314$ on 350M; $p=0.539$ on 700M), while Modular RAG bypasses retrieval for most queries ($0.0218 \pm 0.0628$ Token F1) under our unconstrained, generator-driven prompt routing.
    \item \textbf{Metric-Dependent Scale Effect Within the LFM2 Family}: Under Naive RAG, LFM2-700M has lower mean Token F1 than LFM2-350M ($0.0727 \pm 0.1055$ vs $0.1107 \pm 0.2130$; mean difference $\bar{d}=0.0380$, paired $t$-test $p < 0.001$, permutation test $p < 0.001$). Win counts across questions are nearly equal (210 vs 212 questions, sign test $p=0.96$), while token recall is higher for 700M ($0.587$ vs $0.477$, paired $p<0.001$). At matched response length the F1 gap disappears or reverses, so it is associated with answer length and precision; these lexical metrics do not show which model answers correctly more often.
    \item \textbf{Gold-Context Reference Baseline}: Under 100\% ground-truth gold evidence context (Oracle RAG), LFM2-350M achieves $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1, the highest Token F1 of any arm, while generation-side extraction limits persist even under perfect gold evidence. LFM2-700M achieves $0.1163 \pm 0.1402$ Token F1 under Oracle RAG; its mean output length ($355.9 \pm 6680.7$ tokens) is inflated by a single question whose 10 repeated runs each stated the correct answer and then repeated text until 163,840 tokens (about 1,392s per run). The median latency over all LFM2-700M Oracle runs is $0.520$s.
    \item \textbf{Open Source Benchmark Dataset}: We release the 60,000-run master dataset (\texttt{results/csv/benchmark\_results.csv}), raw per-run JSON logs for 56,941 of the 60,000 runs (3,059 LFM2-350M No-RAG logs were not retained), the FAISS vector database index \cite{johnson2019billion}, and implementation code at \openreview.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}: RAG was introduced by \cite{lewis2020retrieval} to combine parametric memory with non-parametric dense retrieval over Wikipedia passages. Recent surveys by \cite{gao2023retrieval} categorize RAG development into three architectural paradigms: Naive RAG (top-$k$ similarity lookup), Advanced RAG (incorporating pre-retrieval query rewriting \cite{ma2023query} and post-retrieval reranking \cite{nogueira2019passage}), and Modular RAG (utilizing dynamic routing, query decomposition, and reciprocal rank fusion \cite{cormack2009reciprocal}).

\textbf{RAG Evaluation and Diagnostic Metrics}: Recent work evaluates RAG pipelines along multiple dimensions, such as retrieval quality, faithfulness, and answer quality \cite{asai2023self, li2024retrieval}. Standard metrics evaluate exact match token boundaries \cite{rajpurkar2016squad}, sentence embedding similarity using \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} \cite{reimers2019sentence, bge2023embedding}, and groundedness. However, existing comparative studies evaluate almost exclusively on models above 7 billion parameters. Our work extends empirical RAG evaluation into the sub-1B parameter domain.

\textbf{Sub-1B Language Models}: Compact architectures such as Liquid AI's LFM2 family \cite{liquid2024lfm} are engineered for high inference throughput on resource-constrained edge hardware. Understanding how RAG architectural choices affect these specific models is crucial for effective edge AI deployment.

\section{Model Architectures and Target Hardware Setup}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Liquid AI LFM2 Model Architecture Rationale}
In this benchmark, we specifically select Liquid AI's LFM2 (Liquid Foundation Model 2) architecture family \cite{liquid2024lfm}. Originating from foundational work on Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) dynamical systems \cite{hasani2020liquid}, LFM2 combines gated short convolution blocks with lightweight grouped-query attention (GQA) layers \cite{liquid2024lfm}. Unlike standard dense Transformer architectures that rely exclusively on quadratic self-attention across all layers, this hybrid design enables efficient long-context processing (32K context window) with reduced VRAM consumption and high execution throughput on resource-constrained edge hardware.

We evaluate two specific scale configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LFM2-350M} (\texttt{hf.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-350M-GGUF:Q4\_K\_M}): 350 Million parameters, 4-bit GGUF quantization \cite{liquid2024lfm}.
    \item \textbf{LFM2-700M} (\texttt{hf.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-700M-GGUF:Q4\_K\_M}): 700 Million parameters, 4-bit GGUF quantization \cite{liquid2024lfm}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Target Hardware and Inference Configuration Environment}
All benchmark runs were executed locally on a dedicated host laptop:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Processor (CPU)}: AMD Ryzen 7 6800H with Radeon Graphics (8 Cores, 16 Threads, 3.20 GHz base clock)
    \item \textbf{Dedicated Graphics (GPU)}: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Laptop GPU (4GB GDDR6 VRAM, CUDA 12.1 acceleration)
    \item \textbf{Integrated Graphics}: AMD Radeon 680M Graphics
    \item \textbf{Operating System}: Microsoft Windows 11 Home Single Language 64-bit (Build 26200)
    \item \textbf{Execution Stack}: Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.5.1+cu121, Local Ollama Model Server, FAISS (CPU, exact \texttt{IndexFlatIP} over L2-normalised embeddings) \cite{johnson2019billion}
    \item \textbf{Inference Decoding Parameters}: Greedy decoding ($\text{temperature}=0.0$) with a fixed random seed ($\text{seed}=42$), passed with every request. All other sampling options were left at the Ollama server and model defaults, and our code set no maximum generation length (\texttt{rag/generator.py}).
\end{itemize}

\section{System Architecture and RAG Pipeline Arms}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{rag_architecture_bw.jpg}
    \caption{System Architecture diagram of the SLM RAG Benchmark framework (\openreview). The framework orchestrates 5 parallel RAG architectural arms across 600 HotpotQA Distractor queries \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, evaluating LFM2-350M and LFM2-700M \cite{liquid2024lfm} on an NVIDIA RTX 3050 GPU.}
    \label{fig:rag_architecture}
\end{figure}

Our open-source benchmark framework (\openreview) implements five standardized RAG pipeline arms in the \texttt{rag/} package, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:rag_architecture}:

\subsection{No-RAG Baseline Arm}
The No-RAG arm (\texttt{rag/no\_rag/retriever.py}) passes the raw user query directly to the LLM generator without appending any external document context. This arm measures the model's unassisted parametric memory \cite{mallen2023when}.

\subsection{Oracle RAG Arm}
The Oracle RAG arm (\texttt{rag/oracle/retriever.py}) bypasses vector indexing and injects the ground-truth evidence passages directly into the prompt context block. The prompt contains exactly the two gold passages of each question and no distractor passages. This establishes a gold-context reference baseline to isolate in-context answer extraction given 100\% gold passage recall.

\subsection{Naive RAG Arm}
The Naive RAG arm (\texttt{rag/naive/retriever.py}) embeds document passages into a 384-dimensional vector space using \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} \cite{bge2023embedding}. Vectors are indexed in an exact (flat) FAISS inner-product index \cite{johnson2019billion} stored in \texttt{vector\_db/faiss\_index/}. At inference time, the pipeline retrieves the top-$k$ ($k=3$) passages via cosine similarity lookup \cite{lewis2020retrieval} and formats them into a standardized context prompt block.

\subsection{Advanced RAG Arm}
The Advanced RAG arm (\texttt{rag/advanced/retriever.py}) executes a three-stage retrieval strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Query Rewriting}: The evaluated model itself rephrases the input query as a search query \cite{ma2023query}. The rewritten query is used for retrieval and reranking, and the original query for answer generation.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Retrieval}: Up to 12 candidates from FAISS dense search \cite{johnson2019billion} and up to 12 from a TF-IDF sparse index are fused by weighted score fusion (dense weight 0.7), and the top 12 are kept.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Encoder Reranking}: The candidates are reranked using the \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-base} cross-encoder model \cite{nogueira2019passage, bge2023embedding}, and the top-$k$ ($k=3$) highest-scoring passages are passed to the generator.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Modular RAG Arm}
The Modular RAG arm (\texttt{rag/modular/retriever.py}) implements dynamic pipeline orchestration:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Routing}: The generator is asked whether the query needs retrieval and to answer ``yes'' or ``no''. A reply beginning with ``no'' delegates the query to the No-RAG arm; ``yes'' and unparseable replies proceed to retrieval.
    \item \textbf{Sub-query Generation}: The generator writes two search queries related to the question \cite{gao2023retrieval}.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval}: The original query and each sub-query are searched against the FAISS index \cite{johnson2019billion}.
    \item \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}: The retrieved lists are merged with RRF \cite{cormack2009reciprocal}, and the top-$k$ ($k=3$) passages are passed to the generator.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Corpus Ingestion}
We constructed the benchmark dataset by extracting a 600-question subset from the HotpotQA Distractor development set \cite{yang2018hotpotqa} (\texttt{data/benchmark/eval\_set.json}) consisting of its first 600 questions in file order. The subset contains 486 bridge and 114 comparison questions, compared with 5,918 bridge and 1,487 comparison questions in the full split. The subset is deterministic: \texttt{ingest\_hotpotqa.py --sample-size 600} reproduces it by keeping the first 600 questions in file order, and the script takes no seed. We fixed the evaluation set at 600 questions because the full split would require $7{,}405 \times 2 \times 5 \times 10 = 740{,}500$ runs under our design, which is not feasible on the local hardware. The retrieval corpus contains the context paragraphs of all 7,405 development questions (gold and distractor paragraphs), one passage per unique Wikipedia title: 66,581 titles. The index is built directly from the raw HotpotQA JSON (\texttt{utils/db.py}) and contains all 66,581 titles in 67,527 chunks. The plain-text copy written to \texttt{documents/hotpotqa\_corpus/} holds 66,574 files because seven titles map to the same file name once non-alphanumeric characters are replaced and case is ignored (five differ only in case, two only in punctuation); this copy is not used to build the index. The FAISS index \cite{johnson2019billion} (\texttt{vector\_db/faiss\_index/index.faiss} and \texttt{chunks.npy}) was constructed using 1500-character chunks with 150-character overlap.

\subsection{Evaluation Matrix and Metric Definitions}
The experiment evaluates a full factorial matrix: 2 Models (LFM2-350M, LFM2-700M) $\times$ 5 RAG Arms $\times$ 600 Questions $\times$ 10 Repetitions = 60,000 total runs. Evaluated under deterministic decoding ($\text{temperature}=0.0$, fixed seed 42), the 10 repeated executions per question serve to characterize runtime/server latency variability and detect rare execution failures. Under this setup, the 10 repetitions produced identical response text in all 6,000 model-arm-question groups, so the 60,000 runs contain 6,000 distinct generations; the repetitions measure latency variability only, and quality is analysed at the question level. For each run, we measure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Token F1 ($\mu \pm \sigma$)}: Lexical token overlap between model response and ground-truth answer \cite{rajpurkar2016squad}.
    \item \textbf{Exact Match (EM)}: Binary exact match indicator ($0.0$ or $1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Token Precision and Token Recall}: Token precision is the fraction of response tokens that also appear in the ground-truth answer, and token recall is the fraction of ground-truth tokens that appear in the response \cite{adlakha2024evaluating}. Token F1 is their harmonic mean.
    \item \textbf{Document-level IR metrics}: MRR@3, Hit@1, Hit@3, Recall@3, and nDCG@3, computed against the two gold supporting documents of each question.
    \item \textbf{Cosine Semantic Similarity}: Embedding similarity using \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} \cite{reimers2019sentence, bge2023embedding}.
    \item \textbf{Answer-Term Retrieval Hit Rate@3}: Binary indicator ($1.0$ or $0.0$) of whether at least one retrieved passage contains at least 15\% of the content terms of the gold answer string. It does not identify gold supporting documents; document-level retrieval is measured by the IR metrics in Table \ref{tab:ir_metrics}.
    \item \textbf{Groundedness}: Fraction of the response's content (non-stopword) tokens that appear in the retrieved context.
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency}: Total end-to-end processing time in seconds (reported as mean $\pm$ std as well as median and interquartile range for skewed distributions).
    \item \textbf{Output Generation Tokens}: Tokens generated in the final answer call (routing, rewriting, and sub-query calls are excluded).
    \item \textbf{Explicit Refusal Rate}: Frequency of model refusal or abstention outputs.
\end{itemize}
These metrics answer different questions. Exact match rewards an output identical to the reference answer. Token precision and token recall are the two components of Token F1, so they show whether a score difference comes from extra words (precision) or from missing answer words (recall). Cosine similarity assesses semantic similarity beyond token overlap. Groundedness assesses whether the response is supported by the retrieved context. Document-level IR metrics assess retrieval independently of generation, and latency and token counts assess operational cost. The metrics are not redundant in our data: for the Naive RAG scale comparison, paired $t$-tests show that Token F1, precision, exact match, and groundedness favour LFM2-350M, token recall favours LFM2-700M, and cosine similarity shows no difference (Section \ref{sec:paired_recall_em}). Retrieval-level metrics matter most for Modular RAG, where retrieval was bypassed in most runs, so end-to-end F1 alone cannot separate a routing failure from a generation failure.

\subsection{Statistical Significance Methodology}
To evaluate whether observed performance differences between RAG arms and parameter scales are statistically significant, we explicitly connect the descriptive metrics in Table \ref{tab:main_results} (computed over all 60,000 raw executions) with our inferential statistical framework: for each query, the 10 repeated executions under deterministic decoding are aggregated into a single question-level estimate $\bar{x}_q$. Inferential hypothesis testing is then performed across the $N=600$ independent benchmark questions ($d_q = \bar{x}_{q,A} - \bar{x}_{q,B}$). Each question contributes one matched pair, so paired $t$-tests over all questions have $N-1=599$ degrees of freedom (the 425-question refusal-free subset in Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion} has 424). We report paired $t$-tests along with 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) on the mean paired difference $\bar{d}$, accompanied by non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (zero differences excluded) as robustness checks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scale Comparison Within LFM2 (350M vs. 700M Naive RAG)}: LFM2-350M ($0.1107$) achieves a significantly higher mean Token F1 than LFM2-700M ($0.0727$) under Naive RAG at the question level ($\bar{d} = 0.0380$, 95\% CI $[0.0222, 0.0538]$, paired $t=4.7145, p=3.017 \times 10^{-6}, p < 0.001$; sign-flip permutation test with 100,000 draws, $p<10^{-4}$). The rank-based tests do not agree: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives $W=40896.5, p=0.137$, and the two models win nearly equal numbers of questions (350M on 210, 700M on 212, 178 ties; sign test $p=0.96$; median question-level difference $0$). The mean difference therefore comes from the size of the wins rather than their number: the mean margin is $0.180$ on the questions LFM2-350M wins and $0.071$ on those LFM2-700M wins. On the questions LFM2-350M wins, its responses average $48.8$ generated tokens against $108.7$ for LFM2-700M; on the questions LFM2-700M wins, the lengths are similar ($87.7$ tokens for LFM2-350M and $80.7$ for LFM2-700M). With $N=600$ paired questions, this comparison has post hoc power of approximately $0.997$ at two-sided $\alpha=0.05$ for the observed effect ($d_z \approx 0.19$). Power is $0.96$ for $d_z=0.15$ and $0.69$ for $d_z=0.10$. These figures support the claim that the benchmark is adequately powered for the observed effect size; they do not extend to other tasks or to smaller effects.
    \item \textbf{Naive vs. Advanced RAG (LFM2-350M)}: While Naive RAG ($0.1107$) achieves a slightly higher numerical mean than Advanced RAG ($0.1017$), the question-level paired difference ($\bar{d} = 0.0089$, 95\% CI $[-0.0085, 0.0264]$, $t=1.0077, p=0.314$; Wilcoxon $W=34329.0, p=0.862$) is not statistically significant, and the same holds on LFM2-700M ($0.0755$ vs $0.0727$, $p=0.539$). Retrieval metrics are consistent with this result. On LFM2-350M, Advanced RAG loses the gold document from the top 3 on 42 questions where Naive RAG retrieves it and gains it on 14 (exact McNemar $p=0.0002$); document-level MRR@3 falls from $0.8872$ to $0.8439$ (paired difference $0.043$, 95\% CI $[0.017, 0.070]$; Table \ref{tab:ir_metrics}), and Answer-Term Hit Rate@3 from $0.7491$ to $0.6869$. On LFM2-700M there is no such loss (21 lost, 18 gained, $p=0.75$). The dense index, sparse index, and reranker are identical at both scales, so the difference between the two Advanced rows can only come from the rewritten queries, which each model writes itself. LFM2-350M rewrites average $50.5$ words (median 50), against $16.3$ words for the original questions, and $88.5\%$ contain line breaks; LFM2-700M rewrites average $26.0$ words (median 17), and $33.8\%$ contain line breaks. With the LFM2-700M rewrites, Advanced RAG finds a gold document on 45 questions that the LFM2-350M rewrites miss, against 20 the other way (exact McNemar $p=0.003$; MRR@3 $0.8939$ vs $0.8439$, paired $p<0.001$). We did not ablate the individual stages, so we cannot say how much of the remaining Advanced-versus-Naive difference comes from the sparse fusion or the reranker.
    \item \textbf{Token recall and exact match}: Question-level paired comparisons (600 questions, each averaged over 10 runs) give token recall of $0.486$ for Advanced RAG and $0.477$ for Naive RAG on LFM2-350M (Advanced minus Naive $\bar{d}=+0.0089$, 95\% CI $[-0.0255, 0.0434]$, $p=0.610$), and exact match of $0.0283$ for Advanced and $0.0367$ for Naive ($\bar{d}=-0.0083$, 95\% CI $[-0.0240, 0.0074]$, $p=0.298$). Neither metric shows a significant difference, consistent with the F1 result. Scale comparisons under the same procedure are reported in Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Empirical Baseline: Unassisted Parametric Performance}
Under the No-RAG baseline arm, both LFM2-350M and LFM2-700M achieve exactly $0.0000$ Exact Match and low Token F1 scores ($0.0147 \pm 0.0290$ and $0.0149 \pm 0.0292$ respectively). This demonstrates that unassisted generation performs poorly on this benchmark under the evaluated inference setup, while external document evidence substantially improves factual answer quality on multi-hop queries \cite{gao2023retrieval}.

\subsection{Naive RAG Trade-off vs Multi-Stage Complexity}
Single-stage Naive RAG achieves the best observed quality-latency trade-off ($0.1107 \pm 0.2130$ Token F1, $0.0367$ Exact Match) on LFM2-350M with the lowest mean latency of the retrieval-based arms ($0.581 \pm 0.209$s). In contrast:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Advanced RAG}: Incorporating query rewriting \cite{ma2023query}, hybrid retrieval, and cross-encoder reranking \cite{nogueira2019passage} yields a Token F1 of $0.1017 \pm 0.1948$ on 350M, but increases mean latency to $1.460 \pm 0.301$s (a 2.5$\times$ latency overhead without statistically significant F1 gain). The added time comes from the rewrite call ($0.544$s), the larger hybrid retrieval ($0.141$s versus $0.046$s for Naive RAG), and reranking ($0.242$s); answer generation takes the same time in both arms ($0.532$s versus $0.535$s).
    \item \textbf{Modular RAG Implementation Breakdown}: In our unconstrained, generator-driven Modular RAG implementation, the routing step bypasses retrieval for most queries, which drives the low scores ($0.0218 \pm 0.0628$ Token F1 on 350M, $0.0157 \pm 0.0304$ Token F1 on 700M) and high latency overhead on 700M (mean $4.567 \pm 76.593$s, median $1.401$s; Figure \ref{fig:latency_groundedness}). The mean is driven by one question whose 10 repeated runs each took about 1,879s because the sub-query generation call ran away (about 1,875s and about 163,840 generated tokens per run; 164,217 across all calls, against 375 for the final answer), the same failure seen under Oracle RAG (Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion}). The routing step asks the generator a yes/no question (does this query need retrieval?). Inspection of the routing outputs shows that the generator almost always answers a bare ``No'', so retrieval is not triggered:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For \textbf{LFM2-350M}, the router selected \texttt{route: "no\_rag"} in 5,660 out of 6,000 runs ($94.33\%$), attempting unassisted generation and bypassing vector search. Document retrieval was triggered in only 340 runs ($5.67\%$).
        \item For \textbf{LFM2-700M}, the router selected \texttt{route: "no\_rag"} in 5,830 out of 6,000 runs ($97.17\%$), triggering vector retrieval in only 170 runs ($2.83\%$).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Routing that relies on the generator's own judgement, as in the self-routing approach of \cite{li2024retrieval}, depends on the generator following the routing instruction reliably. In our evaluated setup, unconstrained prompt-based routing leads to systematic retrieval bypassing. We did not test constrained decoding or external classifier routers, so this result is a limitation of unconstrained prompt routing rather than evidence against Modular RAG in general. Applying constrained decoding or external classifier routers is the essential next step for making modular architectures viable on sub-1B models.

\section{Detailed Discussion: Metric-Dependent Scale Effect Within the LFM2 Family}
\label{sec:scale_inversion}

A key observation within the evaluated Liquid AI LFM2 model family is that, holding the model family, tokenizer, Q4\_K\_M quantization, prompts, and inference configuration constant, LFM2-350M achieves higher Token F1 than LFM2-700M under Naive RAG ($0.1107$ vs $0.0727$) and Advanced RAG ($0.1017$ vs $0.0755$). This advantage does not hold for token recall, where LFM2-700M is higher under Naive RAG ($0.587$ vs $0.477$) and Oracle RAG ($0.831$ vs $0.690$). We therefore describe this as a metric-dependent scale effect rather than a uniform inversion. The comparison covers two models from one family on one benchmark, so it does not establish a general scaling law. Holding the model family constant provides a controlled within-family comparison that reduces architectural confounding, although empirical differences between model scales cannot be attributed solely to parameter count (as checkpoint-level training dynamics or alignment differences may contribute). Generalization across other sub-1B families remains an open question for future work.

Under Oracle RAG (where 100\% ground-truth gold evidence passages are directly injected into the prompt context), LFM2-350M achieves its highest score across all arms: $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1. Under perfect gold evidence, LFM2-350M achieves higher Token F1 than LFM2-700M ($0.1163 \pm 0.1402$), although LFM2-700M has higher token recall ($0.831$ vs $0.690$), so this comparison does not establish which model answers more often correctly.

To describe how the two scales differ under these configurations, we examine three observations from our generation logs and test each against the data. The statistics added in this revision are produced by \texttt{evaluation/revision\_analyses.py} and stored in \texttt{results/csv/revision\_evidence.csv}.

\subsection{Empirical Observation 1: Refusal Rate Disparity and Latency Distribution}
Across all arms, LFM2-350M refuses between $16.50\%$ (Naive RAG) and $22.00\%$ (Advanced RAG) of runs, indicating a baseline model-level abstention behavior rather than arm-specific calibration. LFM2-700M refuses $17.67\%$ of Naive RAG runs and $14.33\%$ of Advanced RAG runs, with a lower rate under Oracle RAG ($6.33\%$) and higher rates under No-RAG ($29.33\%$) and Modular RAG ($29.00\%$). Refusal does not explain the Naive RAG F1 gap: on the 425 questions that neither model refuses, LFM2-350M still scores higher ($0.1378$ vs $0.0888$; $\bar{d}=0.049$, 95\% CI $[0.028, 0.070]$, paired $p<0.001$; Wilcoxon $p=0.058$). LFM2-700M Oracle runs also show one generation-side failure: while the median latency is $0.520$s (IQR $[0.410\text{s}, 0.673\text{s}]$), all 10 runs of one question ($0.17\%$ of runs) generated 163,840 tokens each in a single call. The response states the correct answer (Tumwater, Washington) in its first two sentences and then repeats text until generation stops, so the evaluator classifies these runs as degenerate (repetition loop) and we score them as zero. They took about 1,392s each and inflate the mean latency to $2.918 \pm 56.772$s. Our code set no generation-length limit (Section \ref{sec:models}), and we did not log the server-side cause. Generation-side stability limitations therefore remain even under gold context.

\subsection{Empirical Observation 2: Output Style and Verbosity Confounders}
Across all Naive RAG responses, LFM2-350M generates $67.2 \pm 58.6$ tokens and LFM2-700M $98.5 \pm 75.4$ tokens. Gold answers are short ($2.4$ tokens on average after SQuAD-style normalisation; $84\%$ have at most three), so Token F1 \cite{rajpurkar2016squad} is dominated by precision, which falls as responses grow. After the same normalisation, LFM2-700M responses contain $1.48$ times as many tokens as LFM2-350M responses but only $1.24$ times as many matching answer tokens, which lowers its precision ($0.044$ vs $0.086$) and F1 while raising its recall ($0.587$ vs $0.477$). To test whether length accounts for the gap, we compared the two models within deciles of response length (generated tokens; deciles pooled over both models' question-level lengths): the Naive RAG F1 difference falls from $+0.038$ to $-0.006$ (95\% bootstrap CI over questions $[-0.020, 0.007]$). A regression of the per-question F1 difference on the log ratio of response lengths gives $-0.016$ (95\% bootstrap CI $[-0.025, -0.007]$) at equal length. Under Oracle RAG, the corresponding estimates are $-0.016$ (95\% CI $[-0.030, -0.002]$) and $-0.013$ (95\% CI $[-0.025, -0.001]$). At matched response length, LFM2-350M therefore no longer scores higher: all four estimates are negative (slightly favouring LFM2-700M), and three of the four have 95\% CIs that exclude zero. Response length is itself an outcome of each model, so this analysis shows that the F1 gap is associated with verbosity; it does not establish that verbosity causes the gap, and it says nothing about multi-hop reasoning ability.

\subsection{Empirical Observation 3: Distractor Sensitivity}
Under Naive RAG on HotpotQA Distractor \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, about half of the retrieved context is non-gold: on average $1.44$ of the three retrieved documents are gold documents (Recall@3 of $0.72$ over the two gold documents per question), and both models receive identical retrieved passages. A plausible hypothesis, consistent with observations in \cite{gao2023retrieval}, is that larger sub-1B models may distribute attention probability more broadly across irrelevant distractor sentences, whereas the compact 350M model maintains tighter focus over local entity spans. Distractors are not needed for the gap, however. The Oracle RAG prompt contains only the two gold passages and no distractors, yet LFM2-350M still scores higher on F1 ($0.1384$ vs $0.1163$; $\bar{d}=0.022$, 95\% CI $[0.007, 0.037]$, paired $p=0.004$; Wilcoxon $p=0.15$), and this gap also disappears or reverses at matched response length (Observation 2). Differential distractor sensitivity therefore remains an untested hypothesis: we did not vary the number of distractor passages or extract attention weights.

\subsection{Gold-Context Reference Baseline Analysis}
Under Oracle RAG, LFM2-350M achieves $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1 compared to LFM2-700M's $0.1163 \pm 0.1402$ Token F1. Gold evidence improves Token F1 over vector retrieval at both scales (350M: $0.1384$ vs $0.1107$, $\bar{d}=0.028$, 95\% CI $[0.009, 0.046]$, $p=0.003$; 700M: $0.1163$ vs $0.0727$, $\bar{d}=0.044$, $p<0.001$), although exact match on LFM2-350M is numerically lower under Oracle than under Naive RAG ($0.0233$ vs $0.0367$). This establishes an upper reference condition for retrieval quality while highlighting that generation-side extraction limits persist even under ideal context.

\subsection{Paired Question-Level Tests for Token Recall and Exact Match}
\label{sec:paired_recall_em}
Following the procedure above, each question's 10 runs are averaged and compared across the 600 questions (paired $t$-test, 95\% CI, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Results are in \texttt{results/csv/paired\_tests\_recall\_em.csv} and \texttt{results/csv/revision\_evidence.csv}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Naive RAG, 350M vs 700M}: Token recall $0.477$ vs $0.587$ (difference $-0.110$, 95\% CI $[-0.142, -0.078]$, $p<0.001$; Wilcoxon $p<0.001$). Exact match $0.0367$ vs $0.0033$ (difference $+0.033$, 95\% CI $[0.018, 0.048]$, $p<0.001$). Token precision $0.086$ vs $0.044$ ($p<0.001$; Wilcoxon $p=0.084$), groundedness $0.728$ vs $0.679$ ($p<0.001$), and cosine similarity $0.591$ vs $0.591$ ($p=0.92$).
    \item \textbf{Oracle RAG, 350M vs 700M}: Token recall $0.690$ vs $0.831$ (difference $-0.141$, 95\% CI $[-0.179, -0.104]$, $p<0.001$). Exact match $0.0233$ vs $0.0067$ (difference $+0.017$, 95\% CI $[0.004, 0.029]$, $p=0.007$).
    \item \textbf{Token F1 in the other arms, 350M vs 700M}: Oracle $\bar{d}=0.022$ ($p=0.004$; Wilcoxon $p=0.15$), Advanced $\bar{d}=0.026$ ($p=0.002$; Wilcoxon $p=0.43$), Modular $\bar{d}=0.006$ ($p=0.017$; Wilcoxon $p=0.28$). As under Naive RAG, the paired $t$-tests favour LFM2-350M while the rank-based tests are not significant.
\end{itemize}
Under Naive RAG, token recall therefore favours LFM2-700M, while exact match, precision, groundedness, and F1 favour LFM2-350M (Tables \ref{tab:main_results} and \ref{tab:precision_recall}). The two lexical proxies for a correct answer also point in opposite directions. The exact-match advantage of LFM2-350M rests on few questions: under Naive RAG, 21 questions are matched exactly only by LFM2-350M and 1 only by LFM2-700M. Conversely, the response contains every token of the reference answer (token recall of $1$) on $50.5\%$ of questions for LFM2-700M against $39.7\%$ for LFM2-350M under Naive RAG, and on $76.7\%$ against $61.2\%$ under Oracle RAG (the LFM2-700M Oracle figure counts the degenerate question). \citet{adlakha2024evaluating} report that token recall agrees more closely with human judgments of correctness than F1 or exact match for verbose instruction-following outputs, but neither metric verifies correctness, so these results do not establish which model answers correctly more often. All 600 questions are included. For the one question whose 10 LFM2-700M Oracle runs were degenerate, exact match is scored as zero.

\subsection{Limitations and Scope}
We explicitly note the scope boundaries of this study: (1) evaluations are conducted within a single architecture family (Liquid AI LFM2), and cross-family generalization to other sub-1B architectures remains to be verified; (2) experiments focus on the HotpotQA Distractor multi-hop QA benchmark, using the first 600 questions of its development split rather than a random sample, so results may not transfer to other tasks, benchmarks, or subsets; (3) dynamic routing was evaluated using an unconstrained generator-driven prompt design; (4) we report document-level MRR, Hit, Recall, and nDCG at rank 3 alongside a custom Answer-Term Hit Rate@3, but we do not use human relevance judgments; (5) all quality metrics are lexical, and we do not perform human or LLM-judge correctness scoring, so the relative correctness of the two model scales cannot be established from these metrics alone; (6) we evaluate no model above 700M parameters, so whether these observations are specific to sub-1B models is untested; and (7) we did not ablate the Advanced RAG stages individually or vary the number of distractor passages, so the explanations in this section are associations rather than causal effects.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this 60,000-run empirical study, we evaluated the impact of RAG pipeline sophistication and parameter scale within Liquid AI's LFM2 sub-1B model family. The Oracle RAG gold-context reference baseline shows that 100\% gold evidence passage injection yields the highest Token F1 ($0.1384$ on LFM2-350M) and token recall, but not the highest exact match ($0.0233$ versus $0.0367$ under Naive RAG), so it serves as a gold-context reference rather than a ceiling, and generation-side extraction bottlenecks remain. Among practical retrieval-based arms, single-stage Naive RAG delivers the best observed quality-latency trade-off ($0.1107$ Token F1) at the lowest latency ($0.581$s). Multi-stage Advanced RAG introduces a 2.5$\times$ latency overhead ($1.460$s) without statistically significant quality gain at either scale ($\bar{d}=0.0089, p=0.314$ on 350M; $p=0.539$ on 700M); on LFM2-350M it also retrieves gold documents less often, together with long, multi-line query rewrites written by the 350M model. Modular RAG bypasses retrieval for most queries ($0.0218$ Token F1) under our unconstrained, generator-driven prompt routing. Furthermore, within the evaluated LFM2 family, LFM2-350M has higher mean Token F1 than LFM2-700M in every retrieval-based arm (Naive RAG: $\bar{d}=0.0380, p < 0.001$; paired $t$-tests $p \le 0.017$ in the other arms), but rank-based tests are not significant in any arm and token recall is higher for LFM2-700M, so the scale effect depends on the metric. Under Naive and Oracle RAG, the F1 gap disappears or reverses when responses of similar length are compared; under Naive RAG, refusal rates do not account for it; and the raw gap is also present under Oracle RAG, where no distractor passages are present. Distractor sensitivity and its attention-level mechanism remain open hypotheses. Future work should ablate the Advanced RAG stages individually, test constrained or classifier-based routing for Modular RAG, vary the number of distractor passages, score correctness with human or LLM judges, and extend the evaluation to other sub-1B families, larger models, and other tasks.
