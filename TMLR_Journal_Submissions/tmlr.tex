\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is widely used to give language models access to external knowledge, but most RAG studies use models with billions of parameters. We report a 120,000-run controlled study of RAG on sub-1B language models from two architecture families: the hybrid convolution-attention LFM2 (350M and 700M) and the dense Transformer SmolLM2 (135M and 360M). Each model is evaluated with five RAG arms (No-RAG, Oracle, Naive, Advanced, and Routed Modular RAG, which combines generator-driven routing with RAG-Fusion) on 600 HotpotQA Distractor questions, with 10 repetitions per question and greedy decoding (the repetitions returned identical outputs for LFM2 but not for SmolLM2), on two laptops with 4GB NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050-class GPUs (one per family). Three findings appear in both families. First, single-stage Naive RAG gives the best quality-latency trade-off: multi-stage Advanced RAG is slower (2.5 times the latency on LFM2-350M) and gives no significant Token F1 gain for any of the four models, and a retrieval-level ablation shows that the query rewrite written by the small model lowers retrieval quality, while the same pipeline without the rewrite retrieves best of all configurations tested. Second, three of the four models cannot judge when they need retrieval: asked whether to retrieve, the LFM2 models answered ``no'' in 94\% and 97\% of Routed Modular RAG runs and SmolLM2-135M in 42\%, declining retrieval on many questions where it would have helped, whereas SmolLM2-360M asked for retrieval in almost every run. Third, even with the two gold passages in the prompt (Oracle RAG), Token F1 stays at or below $0.24$, mainly because token precision is low (at most $0.19$) while token recall reaches $0.50$ to $0.83$. Model scale behaves differently in the two families. Within LFM2, the smaller model has higher Token F1 (Naive RAG $0.111$ versus $0.073$) but lower token recall ($0.477$ versus $0.587$), and the F1 gap disappears or reverses when responses of similar length are compared. Within SmolLM2, the larger model scores higher on every quality metric whenever context is provided (Naive RAG Token F1 $0.151$ versus $0.085$), also at matched response length, so the LFM2 scale inversion is not a general property of sub-1B models. All code, the FAISS index, and the results and raw logs of all 120,000 runs are publicly available at \openreview.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large Language Models (LLMs) with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across factual question answering, reading comprehension, and logical reasoning tasks. However, deploying multi-billion parameter models requires high-memory server GPUs, introducing substantial monetary costs, energy footprint, and privacy risks associated with cloud API dependency. Consequently, Small Language Models (SLMs) containing fewer than 1 billion parameters are increasingly targeted for local, privacy-preserving, and edge deployment on resource-constrained hardware such as laptop GPUs and mobile processors.

Due to their compact parameter footprint, sub-billion parameter models possess limited capacity to memorize multi-hop factual knowledge in their static network weights \citep{mallen2023when, gao2023retrieval}. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \citep{lewis2020retrieval} addresses parametric memory bounds by dynamically fetching relevant external document passages from a corpus and appending them into the model's prompt context.

While sophisticated RAG architectural paradigms incorporating query rewriting \citep{ma2023query}, cross-encoder reranking \citep{nogueira2019passage}, and dynamic sub-query routing with reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009reciprocal} have been developed for multi-billion parameter LLMs \citep{li2024retrieval, asai2023self}, it is unclear whether these abstractions benefit sub-1B language models. The RAG methods reviewed by \citet{gao2023retrieval} are mostly developed and evaluated with multi-billion parameter models, so RAG behaviour in the sub-1B regime is less studied.

To address this gap, we present a full factorial controlled empirical study comprising 120,000 executed benchmark runs: two sub-1B model families, two parameter scales each, and five distinct RAG architectural arms on the HotpotQA Distractor benchmark \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}. The first family is Liquid AI's hybrid LFM2 architecture \citep{liquid2024lfm} (LFM2-350M and LFM2-700M; 60,000 runs). The second is the dense Transformer family SmolLM2 \citep{allal2025smollm2} (SmolLM2-135M and SmolLM2-360M; 60,000 runs), which tests whether the findings transfer to a different architecture (Section \ref{sec:smollm2}). We restrict our conclusions to these two families and this benchmark; larger models and other tasks are left for future work. All evaluations were conducted locally on two Windows 11 laptops with 4GB NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050-class GPUs, one per model family (Section \ref{sec:models}).

Our key empirical findings and contributions are structured as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Best Observed Quality-Latency Trade-Off under Naive RAG}: Single-stage vector retrieval (Naive RAG; \citealp{lewis2020retrieval}) delivers the best observed accuracy-latency trade-off ($0.1107 \pm 0.2130$ Token F1, $0.0367 \pm 0.1880$ Exact Match; \citealp{rajpurkar2016squad}) at the lowest latency among retrieval-based arms ($0.581 \pm 0.209$s) on LFM2-350M \citep{liquid2024lfm}. Multi-stage Advanced RAG takes 2.5 times the latency ($1.460 \pm 0.301$s) without statistically significant quality gain at either scale ($\bar{d}=0.0089, p=0.314$ on 350M; $p=0.539$ on 700M), while Routed Modular RAG (routing plus RAG-Fusion) bypasses retrieval for most queries ($0.0218 \pm 0.0628$ Token F1) under our unconstrained, generator-driven prompt routing.
    \item \textbf{Metric-Dependent Scale Effect Within the LFM2 Family}: Under Naive RAG, LFM2-700M has lower mean Token F1 than LFM2-350M ($0.0727 \pm 0.1055$ vs $0.1107 \pm 0.2130$; mean difference $\bar{d}=0.0380$, paired $t$-test $p < 0.001$, permutation test $p < 0.001$). Win counts across questions are nearly equal (210 vs 212 questions, sign test $p=0.96$), while token recall is higher for 700M ($0.587$ vs $0.477$, paired $p<0.001$). At matched response length the F1 gap disappears or reverses, so it is associated with answer length and precision; these lexical metrics do not show which model answers correctly more often.
    \item \textbf{Gold-Context Reference Baseline}: Under 100\% ground-truth gold evidence context (Oracle RAG), LFM2-350M achieves $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1, the highest Token F1 of any LFM2 arm, while generation-side extraction limits persist even under perfect gold evidence. LFM2-700M achieves $0.1163 \pm 0.1402$ Token F1 under Oracle RAG; its mean output length ($355.9 \pm 6680.7$ tokens) is inflated by a single question whose 10 repeated runs each stated the correct answer and then repeated text until 163,840 tokens (about 1,392s per run). The median latency over all LFM2-700M Oracle runs is $0.520$s.
    \item \textbf{Second Model Family}: Repeating the full protocol on SmolLM2-135M and SmolLM2-360M (60,000 runs), Naive RAG is again the best retrieval-based arm, but with retrieved or gold context the larger model is better on every quality metric, so the LFM2 scale inversion does not transfer. SmolLM2-135M also declines retrieval on questions where it would help, whereas SmolLM2-360M requests retrieval in almost every run (Section \ref{sec:smollm2}).
    \item \textbf{Open Source Benchmark Dataset}: We release the results and raw per-run logs of all 120,000 runs, the FAISS index \citep{johnson2019billion}, and the implementation code at \openreview.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}: RAG was introduced by \citet{lewis2020retrieval} to combine parametric memory with non-parametric dense retrieval over Wikipedia passages. The survey by \citet{gao2023retrieval} categorizes RAG development into three architectural paradigms: Naive RAG (top-$k$ similarity lookup), Advanced RAG, which adds pre-retrieval query rewriting \citep{ma2023query} and post-retrieval reranking \citep{nogueira2019passage}, and Modular RAG, which composes interchangeable modules such as routing, multi-query retrieval with reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009reciprocal}, memory, and task adapters.

\textbf{RAG Evaluation and Diagnostic Metrics}: Recent work evaluates RAG pipelines along multiple dimensions, such as retrieval quality, faithfulness, and answer quality \citep{asai2023self, li2024retrieval}. Standard metrics evaluate exact match token boundaries \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}, sentence embedding similarity using \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} \citep{reimers2019sentence, bge2023embedding}, and groundedness. Most comparative RAG evaluations use models with several billion parameters; our work evaluates RAG pipelines on sub-1B models.

\textbf{Sub-1B Language Models}: Compact model families such as Liquid AI's LFM2 \citep{liquid2024lfm} and SmolLM2 \citep{allal2025smollm2} target local and on-device use. How RAG pipeline choices affect such models matters for deployment on edge hardware.

\section{Model Architectures and Target Hardware Setup}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Liquid AI LFM2 Model Architecture Rationale}
In this benchmark, we specifically select Liquid AI's LFM2 (Liquid Foundation Model 2) architecture family \citep{liquid2024lfm}. Originating from foundational work on Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) dynamical systems \citep{hasani2020liquid}, LFM2 combines gated short convolution blocks with lightweight grouped-query attention (GQA) layers \citep{liquid2024lfm}. Unlike standard dense Transformer architectures that rely exclusively on quadratic self-attention across all layers, this hybrid design is intended for efficient long-context processing (32K context window) with low memory use and high throughput on edge hardware \citep{liquid2024lfm}.

We evaluate two specific scale configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LFM2-350M} (\texttt{hf.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-350M-GGUF:Q4\_K\_M}): 350 Million parameters, 4-bit GGUF quantization \citep{liquid2024lfm}.
    \item \textbf{LFM2-700M} (\texttt{hf.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-700M-GGUF:Q4\_K\_M}): 700 Million parameters, 4-bit GGUF quantization \citep{liquid2024lfm}.
\end{itemize}
As a second family we use SmolLM2 \citep{allal2025smollm2}, a dense Transformer (Llama architecture with grouped-query attention): \textbf{SmolLM2-135M} (\texttt{hf.co/bartowski/SmolLM2-135M-Instruct-GGUF:Q4\_K\_M}) and \textbf{SmolLM2-360M} (\texttt{hf.co/bartowski/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct-GGUF:Q4\_K\_M}), both with the same 4-bit quantization.

Our open-source benchmark framework (\openreview) implements five standardized RAG pipeline arms in the \texttt{rag/} package, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:rag_architecture}:

\subsection{No-RAG Baseline Arm}
The No-RAG arm (\texttt{rag/no\_rag/retriever.py}) passes the raw user query directly to the LLM generator without appending any external document context. This arm measures the model's unassisted parametric memory \citep{mallen2023when}.

\subsection{Oracle RAG Arm}
The Oracle RAG arm (\texttt{rag/oracle/retriever.py}) bypasses vector indexing and injects the ground-truth evidence passages directly into the prompt context block. The prompt contains exactly the two gold passages of each question and no distractor passages. This establishes a gold-context reference baseline to isolate in-context answer extraction given 100\% gold passage recall.

\subsection{Naive RAG Arm}
The Naive RAG arm (\texttt{rag/naive/retriever.py}) embeds document passages into a 384-dimensional vector space using \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} \citep{bge2023embedding}. Vectors are indexed in an exact (flat) FAISS inner-product index \citep{johnson2019billion} stored in \texttt{vector\_db/faiss\_index/}. At inference time, the pipeline retrieves the top-$k$ ($k=3$) passages via cosine similarity lookup \citep{lewis2020retrieval} and formats them into a standardized context prompt block.

\subsection{Advanced RAG Arm}
The Advanced RAG arm (\texttt{rag/advanced/retriever.py}) executes a three-stage retrieval strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Query Rewriting}: The evaluated model itself rephrases the input query as a search query \citep{ma2023query}. The rewritten query is used for retrieval and reranking, and the original query for answer generation.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Retrieval}: Up to 12 candidates from FAISS dense search \citep{johnson2019billion} and up to 12 from a TF-IDF sparse index are fused by weighted score fusion (dense weight 0.7), and the top 12 are kept.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Encoder Reranking}: The candidates are reranked using the \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-base} cross-encoder model \citep{nogueira2019passage, bge2023embedding}, and the top-$k$ ($k=3$) highest-scoring passages are passed to the generator.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Routed Modular RAG Arm}
Modular RAG, as described by \citet{gao2023retrieval}, is a family of configurations built from interchangeable modules (Search, RAG-Fusion, Memory, Routing, Predict, and Task Adapter) rather than one fixed pipeline. Our Routed Modular RAG arm (\texttt{rag/modular/retriever.py}) implements one configuration that combines two of these modules, hence its name: a Routing step in which the generator itself decides whether to retrieve, in the spirit of adaptive retrieval methods where the LLM decides when to search \citep{asai2023self}, and RAG-Fusion, which retrieves with several related queries and merges the results with RRF. Other module combinations are not evaluated. The arm runs as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Routing}: The generator is asked whether the query needs retrieval and to answer ``yes'' or ``no''. A reply beginning with ``no'' delegates the query to the No-RAG arm; ``yes'' and unparseable replies proceed to retrieval.
    \item \textbf{Sub-query Generation}: The generator writes two search queries related to the question \citep{gao2023retrieval}.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval}: The original query and each sub-query are searched against the FAISS index \citep{johnson2019billion}.
    \item \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}: The retrieved lists are merged with RRF \citep{cormack2009reciprocal}, and the top-$k$ ($k=3$) passages are passed to the generator.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Corpus Ingestion}
We constructed the benchmark dataset by extracting a 600-question subset from the HotpotQA Distractor development set \citep{yang2018hotpotqa} (\texttt{data/benchmark/eval\_set.json}) consisting of its first 600 questions in file order. The subset contains 486 bridge and 114 comparison questions, compared with 5,918 bridge and 1,487 comparison questions in the full split. The subset is deterministic: \texttt{ingest\_hotpotqa.py --sample-size 600} reproduces it by keeping the first 600 questions in file order, and the script takes no seed. We fixed the evaluation set at 600 questions because the full split would require $7{,}405 \times 2 \times 5 \times 10 = 740{,}500$ runs under our design, which is not feasible on the local hardware. The retrieval corpus contains the context paragraphs of all 7,405 development questions (gold and distractor paragraphs), one passage per unique Wikipedia title: 66,581 titles. The index is built directly from the raw HotpotQA JSON (\texttt{utils/db.py}) and contains all 66,581 titles in 67,527 chunks. The plain-text copy written to \texttt{documents/hotpotqa\_corpus/} holds 66,574 files because seven titles map to the same file name once non-alphanumeric characters are replaced and case is ignored (five differ only in case, two only in punctuation); this copy is not used to build the index. The FAISS index \citep{johnson2019billion} (\texttt{vector\_db/faiss\_index/index.faiss} and \texttt{chunks.npy}) was constructed using 1500-character chunks with 150-character overlap.

\subsection{Evaluation Matrix and Metric Definitions}
The experiment evaluates a full factorial matrix for each model family: 2 models $\times$ 5 RAG arms $\times$ 600 questions $\times$ 10 repetitions = 60,000 runs per family (LFM2-350M and LFM2-700M; SmolLM2-135M and SmolLM2-360M), or 120,000 runs in total. Evaluated under greedy decoding ($\text{temperature}=0.0$, fixed seed 42), the 10 repeated executions per question serve to characterize runtime/server latency variability and detect rare execution failures. For LFM2, the 10 repetitions produced identical response text in all 6,000 model-arm-question groups (for SmolLM2 they did not; Section \ref{sec:smollm2}), so the 60,000 LFM2 runs contain 6,000 distinct generations; the repetitions measure latency variability only, and quality is analysed at the question level. For each run, we measure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Token F1 ($\mu \pm \sigma$)}: Lexical token overlap between model response and ground-truth answer \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}.
    \item \textbf{Exact Match (EM)}: Binary exact match indicator ($0.0$ or $1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Token Precision and Token Recall}: Token precision is the fraction of response tokens that also appear in the ground-truth answer, and token recall is the fraction of ground-truth tokens that appear in the response \citep{adlakha2024evaluating}. Token F1 is their harmonic mean.
    \item \textbf{Document-level IR metrics}: MRR@3, Hit@1, Hit@3, Recall@3, and nDCG@3, computed against the two gold supporting documents of each question.
    \item \textbf{Cosine Semantic Similarity}: Embedding similarity using \texttt{BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5} \citep{reimers2019sentence, bge2023embedding}.
    \item \textbf{Answer-Term Hit Rate@3}: Binary indicator ($1.0$ or $0.0$) of whether at least one retrieved passage contains at least 15\% of the content terms of the gold answer string. It does not identify gold supporting documents; the document-level IR metrics in Table \ref{tab:ir_metrics} are our primary retrieval evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Groundedness}: Fraction of the response's content (non-stopword) tokens that appear in the retrieved context.
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency}: Total end-to-end processing time in seconds (reported as mean $\pm$ std as well as median and interquartile range for skewed distributions).
    \item \textbf{Output Generation Tokens}: Tokens generated in the final answer call (routing, rewriting, and sub-query calls are excluded).
    \item \textbf{Explicit Refusal Rate}: Frequency of model refusal or abstention outputs.
\end{itemize}
These metrics answer different questions. Exact match rewards an output identical to the reference answer. Token precision and token recall are the two components of Token F1, so they show whether a score difference comes from extra words (precision) or from missing answer words (recall). Cosine similarity assesses semantic similarity beyond token overlap. Groundedness measures the share of the response's content words that appear in the retrieved context. Document-level IR metrics assess retrieval independently of generation, and latency and token counts assess operational cost. The metrics are not redundant in our data: for the Naive RAG scale comparison, paired $t$-tests show that Token F1, precision, exact match, and groundedness favour LFM2-350M, token recall favours LFM2-700M, and cosine similarity shows no difference (Section \ref{sec:paired_recall_em}). Retrieval-level metrics matter most for Routed Modular RAG, where retrieval was bypassed in most runs, so end-to-end F1 alone cannot separate a routing failure from a generation failure.

\subsection{Statistical Significance Methodology}
To evaluate whether observed performance differences between RAG arms and parameter scales are statistically significant, we explicitly connect the descriptive metrics in Table \ref{tab:main_results} (computed over all 60,000 LFM2 runs) with our inferential statistical framework: for each query, the 10 repeated executions are averaged into a single question-level estimate $\bar{x}_q$. Inferential hypothesis testing is then performed across the $N=600$ independent benchmark questions ($d_q = \bar{x}_{q,A} - \bar{x}_{q,B}$). Each question contributes one matched pair, so paired $t$-tests over all questions have $N-1=599$ degrees of freedom (the 425-question and 572-question refusal-free subsets in Sections \ref{sec:scale_inversion} and \ref{sec:smollm2} have 424 and 571). We report paired $t$-tests along with 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) on the mean paired difference $\bar{d}$, accompanied by non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (zero differences excluded) as robustness checks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scale Comparison Within LFM2 (350M vs. 700M Naive RAG)}: LFM2-350M ($0.1107$) achieves a significantly higher mean Token F1 than LFM2-700M ($0.0727$) under Naive RAG at the question level ($\bar{d} = 0.0380$, 95\% CI $[0.0222, 0.0538]$, paired $t=4.7145, p=3.017 \times 10^{-6}, p < 0.001$; sign-flip permutation test with 100,000 draws, $p<10^{-4}$). The rank-based tests do not agree: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives $W=40896.5, p=0.137$, and the two models win nearly equal numbers of questions (350M on 210, 700M on 212, 178 ties; sign test $p=0.96$; median question-level difference $0$). The mean difference therefore comes from the size of the wins rather than their number: the mean margin is $0.180$ on the questions LFM2-350M wins and $0.071$ on those LFM2-700M wins. On the questions LFM2-350M wins, its responses average $48.8$ generated tokens against $108.7$ for LFM2-700M; on the questions LFM2-700M wins, the lengths are similar ($87.7$ tokens for LFM2-350M and $80.7$ for LFM2-700M). With $N=600$ paired questions and two-sided $\alpha=0.05$, the design has power $0.96$ to detect a standardised paired effect of $d_z=0.15$ and $0.69$ for $d_z=0.10$; the observed effect is $d_z \approx 0.19$ (post hoc power $0.997$, which follows from the $p$-value and adds no further evidence).
    \item \textbf{Naive vs. Advanced RAG (LFM2-350M)}: While Naive RAG ($0.1107$) achieves a slightly higher numerical mean than Advanced RAG ($0.1017$), the question-level paired difference ($\bar{d} = 0.0089$, 95\% CI $[-0.0085, 0.0264]$, $t=1.0077, p=0.314$; Wilcoxon $W=34329.0, p=0.862$) is not statistically significant, and the same holds on LFM2-700M ($0.0755$ vs $0.0727$, $p=0.539$). Retrieval metrics are consistent with this result. On LFM2-350M, Advanced RAG loses the gold document from the top 3 on 42 questions where Naive RAG retrieves it and gains it on 14 (exact McNemar $p=0.0002$); document-level MRR@3 falls from $0.8872$ to $0.8439$ (paired difference $0.043$, 95\% CI $[0.017, 0.070]$; Table \ref{tab:ir_metrics}), and Answer-Term Hit Rate@3 from $0.7491$ to $0.6869$. On LFM2-700M there is no such loss (21 lost, 18 gained, $p=0.75$). The dense index, sparse index, and reranker are identical at both scales, so the difference between the two Advanced rows can only come from the rewritten queries, which each model writes itself. LFM2-350M rewrites average $50.5$ words (median 50), against $16.3$ words for the original questions, and $88.5\%$ contain line breaks; LFM2-700M rewrites average $26.0$ words (median 17), and $33.8\%$ contain line breaks. With the LFM2-700M rewrites, Advanced RAG finds a gold document on 45 questions that the LFM2-350M rewrites miss, against 20 the other way (exact McNemar $p=0.003$; MRR@3 $0.8939$ vs $0.8439$, paired $p<0.001$). A retrieval-level ablation identifies the rewrite as the cause of this retrieval loss (Table \ref{tab:retrieval_ablation}). Because retrieval is deterministic, we replayed each stage on the exact rewritten queries logged in the original runs, without any new generation; the full configuration reproduces the logged Advanced top-3 passages for all 600 questions at both scales. On LFM2-350M, searching with the rewrite alone lowers Hit@3 from $0.948$ (Naive) to $0.703$ and MRR@3 from $0.891$ to $0.628$; reranking recovers most of this loss ($0.902$ and $0.848$ for the full arm). Removing the rewrite while keeping hybrid retrieval and reranking gives the best retrieval of all configurations (Hit@3 $0.975$, MRR@3 $0.956$), better than Naive RAG (gold document gained on 21 questions and lost on 5, exact McNemar $p=0.002$; MRR@3 difference $+0.065$, paired $p<0.001$). On LFM2-700M the rewrite also lowers retrieval (Hit@3 $0.872$ with the rewrite alone), and reranking recovers it to the Naive RAG level ($0.945$ vs $0.948$). We did not measure the answer-level effect of each stage (Section \ref{sec:limitations}).
    \item \textbf{Token recall and exact match}: Question-level paired comparisons (600 questions, each averaged over 10 runs) give token recall of $0.486$ for Advanced RAG and $0.477$ for Naive RAG on LFM2-350M (Advanced minus Naive $\bar{d}=+0.0089$, 95\% CI $[-0.0255, 0.0434]$, $p=0.610$), and exact match of $0.0283$ for Advanced and $0.0367$ for Naive ($\bar{d}=-0.0083$, 95\% CI $[-0.0240, 0.0074]$, $p=0.298$). Neither metric shows a significant difference, consistent with the F1 result. Scale comparisons under the same procedure are reported in Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Empirical Baseline: Unassisted Parametric Performance}
Under the No-RAG baseline arm, both LFM2-350M and LFM2-700M achieve exactly $0.0000$ Exact Match and low Token F1 scores ($0.0147 \pm 0.0290$ and $0.0149 \pm 0.0292$ respectively). Unassisted generation therefore performs poorly on this benchmark, and retrieved evidence raises Token F1 substantially (Naive RAG $0.1107$ and $0.0727$).

\subsection{Naive RAG Trade-off vs Multi-Stage Complexity}
Single-stage Naive RAG achieves the best observed quality-latency trade-off ($0.1107 \pm 0.2130$ Token F1, $0.0367$ Exact Match) on LFM2-350M with the lowest mean latency of the retrieval-based arms ($0.581 \pm 0.209$s). In contrast:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Advanced RAG}: Incorporating query rewriting \citep{ma2023query}, hybrid retrieval, and cross-encoder reranking \citep{nogueira2019passage} yields a Token F1 of $0.1017 \pm 0.1948$ on 350M, but increases mean latency to $1.460 \pm 0.301$s (2.5 times the Naive RAG latency, without statistically significant F1 gain). The added time comes from the rewrite call ($0.544$s), the larger hybrid retrieval ($0.141$s versus $0.046$s for Naive RAG), and reranking ($0.242$s); answer generation takes the same time in both arms ($0.532$s versus $0.535$s).
    \item \textbf{Routed Modular RAG}: In our unconstrained, generator-driven Routed Modular RAG implementation, the routing step bypasses retrieval for most queries, which drives the low scores ($0.0218 \pm 0.0628$ Token F1 on 350M, $0.0157 \pm 0.0304$ Token F1 on 700M) and high latency overhead on 700M (mean $4.567 \pm 76.593$s, median $1.401$s; Figure \ref{fig:latency_groundedness}). The mean is driven by one question whose 10 repeated runs each took about 1,879s because the sub-query generation call ran away (about 1,875s and about 163,840 generated tokens per run; 164,217 across all calls, against 375 for the final answer), the same failure seen under Oracle RAG (Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion}). The routing step asks the generator a yes/no question (does this query need retrieval?). Inspection of the routing outputs shows that the generator almost always answers a bare ``No'', so retrieval is not triggered:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For \textbf{LFM2-350M}, the router selected \texttt{route: "no\_rag"} in 5,660 out of 6,000 runs ($94.33\%$), attempting unassisted generation and bypassing vector search. Document retrieval was triggered in only 340 runs ($5.67\%$).
        \item For \textbf{LFM2-700M}, the router selected \texttt{route: "no\_rag"} in 5,830 out of 6,000 runs ($97.17\%$), triggering vector retrieval in only 170 runs ($2.83\%$).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The routing step therefore tests whether a sub-1B model can recognise when it needs external evidence, and the answer in our setup is no. On the questions each model routed away from retrieval (566 for LFM2-350M, 583 for LFM2-700M), answering without retrieval gives a Token F1 of $0.014$ and $0.015$, whereas Naive RAG on the same questions gives $0.108$ and $0.072$; retrieval raises F1 on 299 and 359 of these questions and lowers it on 51 and 47. Both models therefore declined retrieval on many questions where it would have helped. Routing that relies on the generator's own judgement, as in the self-routing approach of \citet{li2024retrieval}, depends on the generator following the routing instruction reliably. In our evaluated setup, unconstrained prompt-based routing leads to systematic retrieval bypassing. We did not test constrained decoding or external classifier routers, so this result is a limitation of unconstrained prompt routing rather than evidence against Modular RAG in general. Constrained decoding would guarantee a valid label but not a correct routing decision; an external classifier router addresses the decision itself and is a natural next step.

\section{Detailed Discussion: Metric-Dependent Scale Effect Within the LFM2 Family}
\label{sec:scale_inversion}

A key observation within the evaluated Liquid AI LFM2 model family is that, holding the model family, tokenizer, Q4\_K\_M quantization, prompts, and inference configuration constant, LFM2-350M achieves higher Token F1 than LFM2-700M under Naive RAG ($0.1107$ vs $0.0727$) and Advanced RAG ($0.1017$ vs $0.0755$). This advantage does not hold for token recall, where LFM2-700M is higher under Naive RAG ($0.587$ vs $0.477$) and Oracle RAG ($0.831$ vs $0.690$). We therefore describe this as a metric-dependent scale effect rather than a uniform inversion. The comparison covers two models from one family on one benchmark, so it does not establish a general scaling law. Holding the model family constant provides a controlled within-family comparison that reduces architectural confounding, although empirical differences between model scales cannot be attributed solely to parameter count (as checkpoint-level training dynamics or alignment differences may contribute). Section \ref{sec:smollm2} tests whether the pattern appears in a second family.

Under Oracle RAG (where 100\% ground-truth gold evidence passages are directly injected into the prompt context), LFM2-350M achieves its highest score across all arms: $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1. Under perfect gold evidence, LFM2-350M achieves higher Token F1 than LFM2-700M ($0.1163 \pm 0.1402$), although LFM2-700M has higher token recall ($0.831$ vs $0.690$), so this comparison does not establish which model answers more often correctly.

To describe how the two scales differ under these configurations, we examine three observations from our generation logs and test each against the data. The statistics added in this revision are produced by \texttt{evaluation/revision\_analyses.py} and stored in \texttt{results/csv/revision\_evidence.csv}.

\subsection{Empirical Observation 1: Refusal Rate Disparity and Latency Distribution}
Across all arms, LFM2-350M refuses between $16.50\%$ (Naive RAG) and $22.00\%$ (Advanced RAG) of runs, suggesting a model-level abstention tendency rather than arm-specific behaviour. LFM2-700M refuses $17.67\%$ of Naive RAG runs and $14.33\%$ of Advanced RAG runs, with a lower rate under Oracle RAG ($6.33\%$) and higher rates under No-RAG ($29.33\%$) and Routed Modular RAG ($29.00\%$). Refusal does not explain the Naive RAG F1 gap: on the 425 questions that neither model refuses, LFM2-350M still scores higher ($0.1378$ vs $0.0888$; $\bar{d}=0.049$, 95\% CI $[0.028, 0.070]$, paired $p<0.001$; Wilcoxon $p=0.058$). LFM2-700M Oracle runs also show one generation-side failure: while the median latency is $0.520$s (IQR $[0.410\text{s}, 0.673\text{s}]$), all 10 runs of one question ($0.17\%$ of runs) generated 163,840 tokens each in a single call. The response states the correct answer (Tumwater, Washington) in its first two sentences and then repeats text until generation stops, so the evaluator classifies these runs as degenerate (repetition loop) and we score them as zero. They took about 1,392s each and inflate the mean latency to $2.918 \pm 56.772$s. Our code set no generation-length limit (Section \ref{sec:models}), and we did not log the server-side cause. Runaway generation can therefore occur even with gold context.

\subsection{Empirical Observation 2: Output Style and Verbosity Confounders}
Across all Naive RAG responses, LFM2-350M generates $67.2 \pm 58.6$ tokens and LFM2-700M $98.5 \pm 75.4$ tokens. Gold answers are short ($2.4$ tokens on average after SQuAD-style normalisation; $84\%$ have at most three), so Token F1 \citep{rajpurkar2016squad} is dominated by precision, which falls as responses grow. After the same normalisation, LFM2-700M responses contain $1.48$ times as many tokens as LFM2-350M responses but only $1.24$ times as many matching answer tokens, which lowers its precision ($0.044$ vs $0.086$) and F1 while raising its recall ($0.587$ vs $0.477$). To test whether length accounts for the gap, we compared the two models within deciles of response length (generated tokens; deciles pooled over both models' question-level lengths): the Naive RAG F1 difference falls from $+0.038$ to $-0.006$ (95\% bootstrap CI over questions $[-0.020, 0.007]$). A regression of the per-question F1 difference on the log ratio of response lengths gives $-0.016$ (95\% bootstrap CI $[-0.025, -0.007]$) at equal length. Under Oracle RAG, the corresponding estimates are $-0.016$ (95\% CI $[-0.030, -0.002]$) and $-0.013$ (95\% CI $[-0.025, -0.001]$). At matched response length, LFM2-350M therefore no longer scores higher: all four estimates are negative (slightly favouring LFM2-700M), and three of the four have 95\% CIs that exclude zero. Response length is itself an outcome of each model, so this analysis shows that the F1 gap is associated with verbosity; it does not establish that verbosity causes the gap, and it says nothing about multi-hop reasoning ability.

\subsection{Empirical Observation 3: Distractor Sensitivity}
Under Naive RAG on HotpotQA Distractor \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, about half of the retrieved context is non-gold: on average $1.44$ of the three retrieved documents are gold documents (Recall@3 of $0.72$ over the two gold documents per question), and both models receive identical retrieved passages. Retrieval quality therefore does not explain the scale gap: under Naive RAG the passages are identical, and under Advanced RAG LFM2-700M retrieves better evidence than LFM2-350M (MRR@3 $0.8939$ vs $0.8439$, Table \ref{tab:ir_metrics}) yet has lower Token F1 ($0.0755$ vs $0.1017$), so the gap arises on the generation side. One hypothesis is that the larger model attends more broadly to irrelevant distractor sentences, whereas the 350M model stays focused on local entity spans. Distractors are not needed for the gap, however. The Oracle RAG prompt contains only the two gold passages and no distractors, yet LFM2-350M still scores higher on F1 ($0.1384$ vs $0.1163$; $\bar{d}=0.022$, 95\% CI $[0.007, 0.037]$, paired $p=0.004$; Wilcoxon $p=0.15$), and this gap also disappears or reverses at matched response length (Observation 2). Differential distractor sensitivity therefore remains an untested hypothesis: we did not vary the number of distractor passages or extract attention weights.

\subsection{Gold-Context Reference Baseline Analysis}
Under Oracle RAG, LFM2-350M achieves $0.1384 \pm 0.1935$ Token F1 compared to LFM2-700M's $0.1163 \pm 0.1402$ Token F1. Gold evidence improves Token F1 over vector retrieval at both scales (350M: $0.1384$ vs $0.1107$, $\bar{d}=0.028$, 95\% CI $[0.009, 0.046]$, $p=0.003$; 700M: $0.1163$ vs $0.0727$, $\bar{d}=0.044$, $p<0.001$), although exact match on LFM2-350M is numerically lower under Oracle than under Naive RAG ($0.0233$ vs $0.0367$). This establishes an upper reference condition for retrieval quality while highlighting that generation-side extraction limits persist even under ideal context.

\subsection{Paired Question-Level Tests for Token Recall and Exact Match}
\label{sec:paired_recall_em}
Following the procedure above, each question's 10 runs are averaged and compared across the 600 questions (paired $t$-test, 95\% CI, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Results are in \texttt{results/csv/paired\_tests\_recall\_em.csv} and \texttt{results/csv/revision\_evidence.csv}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Naive RAG, 350M vs 700M}: Token recall $0.477$ vs $0.587$ (difference $-0.110$, 95\% CI $[-0.142, -0.078]$, $p<0.001$; Wilcoxon $p<0.001$). Exact match $0.0367$ vs $0.0033$ (difference $+0.033$, 95\% CI $[0.018, 0.048]$, $p<0.001$). Token precision $0.086$ vs $0.044$ ($p<0.001$; Wilcoxon $p=0.084$), groundedness $0.728$ vs $0.679$ ($p<0.001$), and cosine similarity $0.591$ vs $0.591$ ($p=0.92$).
    \item \textbf{Oracle RAG, 350M vs 700M}: Token recall $0.690$ vs $0.831$ (difference $-0.141$, 95\% CI $[-0.179, -0.104]$, $p<0.001$). Exact match $0.0233$ vs $0.0067$ (difference $+0.017$, 95\% CI $[0.004, 0.029]$, $p=0.007$).
    \item \textbf{Token F1 in the other arms, 350M vs 700M}: Oracle $\bar{d}=0.022$ ($p=0.004$; Wilcoxon $p=0.15$), Advanced $\bar{d}=0.026$ ($p=0.002$; Wilcoxon $p=0.43$), Routed Modular RAG $\bar{d}=0.006$ ($p=0.017$; Wilcoxon $p=0.28$). As under Naive RAG, the paired $t$-tests favour LFM2-350M while the rank-based tests are not significant.
\end{itemize}
Under Naive RAG, token recall therefore favours LFM2-700M, while exact match, precision, groundedness, and F1 favour LFM2-350M (Tables \ref{tab:main_results} and \ref{tab:precision_recall}). The two lexical proxies for a correct answer also point in opposite directions. The exact-match advantage of LFM2-350M rests on few questions: under Naive RAG, 21 questions are matched exactly only by LFM2-350M and 1 only by LFM2-700M. Conversely, the response contains every token of the reference answer (token recall of $1$) on $50.5\%$ of questions for LFM2-700M against $39.7\%$ for LFM2-350M under Naive RAG, and on $76.7\%$ against $61.2\%$ under Oracle RAG (the LFM2-700M Oracle figure counts the degenerate question). \citet{adlakha2024evaluating} report that token recall agrees more closely with human judgments of correctness than F1 or exact match for verbose instruction-following outputs, but neither metric verifies correctness, so these results do not establish which model answers correctly more often. All 600 questions are included. For the one question whose 10 LFM2-700M Oracle runs were degenerate, exact match is scored as zero.

\subsection{Comparison with Larger Models in Prior Work}
\label{sec:larger_models}
We did not run models above 700M parameters, but prior work with larger models bears on whether our observations are specific to small models. Rewriting queries before retrieval improves retrieval-augmented question answering with a black-box LLM reader, with consistent gains on open-domain and multiple-choice QA \citep{ma2023query}. Self-RAG trains 7B and 13B models to retrieve on demand, deciding through reflection tokens, and reports that they outperform strong LLM and retrieval-augmented baselines \citep{asai2023self}. Self-Route lets recent large LLMs decide by self-reflection whether to answer from retrieved chunks, keeping performance comparable to long-context processing at much lower cost \citep{li2024retrieval}. In our sub-1B models, by contrast, the query rewrite written by the model lowers retrieval quality (Table \ref{tab:retrieval_ablation}), and three of the four models do not decide reliably when retrieval is needed (Sections \ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:smollm2}). This contrast is consistent with these behaviours depending on model capability, but the models, retrievers, prompts, datasets, and metrics differ from ours, so it is not a controlled comparison.

\textbf{Naive RAG remains the best retrieval-based arm.} At both scales, Naive RAG has the highest Token F1 of the retrieval-based arms and the lowest median latency. Advanced RAG gives no significant gain (Advanced minus Naive $\bar{d}=-0.009$, $p=0.15$ on 135M; $\bar{d}=-0.015$, $p=0.09$ on 360M) and again retrieves gold documents less often than Naive RAG (document Hit@3 $0.912$ versus $0.948$ at both scales; the gold document leaves the top 3 on 35 questions and enters it on 13 for SmolLM2-135M, exact McNemar $p=0.002$, and on 38 and 16 for SmolLM2-360M, $p=0.004$). The retrieval-level ablation (Table \ref{tab:retrieval_ablation}) shows the same cause as in LFM2: searching with the rewrite alone lowers Hit@3 from $0.948$ to $0.842$ on SmolLM2-135M and $0.857$ on SmolLM2-360M, reranking recovers part of this loss, and the full configuration reproduces the logged Advanced top-3 passages for all 600 questions for both models. SmolLM2 rewrites vary across repetitions (identical in all 10 runs for 163 questions on SmolLM2-135M and 276 on SmolLM2-360M), so the replay uses the run-1 rewrites. Rewrite length does not explain the loss on its own: in run 1, SmolLM2-135M rewrites average $28.1$ words (median 19) and $7.0\%$ contain line breaks, whereas SmolLM2-360M rewrites average $16.1$ words (median 16), about as long as the original questions ($16.3$ words), and $1.5\%$ contain line breaks, yet they lower Hit@3 by a similar amount. The SmolLM2-360M Advanced RAG latency (median $3.414$s, Table \ref{tab:smollm2}) includes time that the pipeline stages do not explain: hybrid retrieval, which does not involve the language model, took $1.034$s on average against $0.078$s on SmolLM2-135M, and answer generation took $0.995$s against $0.235$s in the same model's Naive RAG runs, at a similar decoding speed (median 257 versus 242 tokens/s) and answer length (median 26 versus 25 tokens); the rewrite took $1.228$s, while reranking took the same time as on SmolLM2-135M ($0.173$s). On SmolLM2-135M, Advanced RAG takes 2.6 times the Naive RAG latency (mean $0.639$s versus $0.247$s; rewrite $0.182$s, hybrid retrieval $0.078$s versus $0.017$s, reranking $0.173$s, answer generation $0.207$s versus $0.231$s), close to the ratio of 2.5 on LFM2-350M. Gold passages raise Token F1 over Naive RAG for both models (SmolLM2-135M: $0.1274$ vs $0.0851$, $\bar{d}=0.042$, 95\% CI $[0.025, 0.059]$; SmolLM2-360M: $0.2397$ vs $0.1514$, $\bar{d}=0.088$, 95\% CI $[0.067, 0.110]$; both paired $p<0.001$), and unlike on LFM2-350M, exact match is also higher under Oracle RAG than under Naive RAG ($0.0247$ vs $0.0198$ and $0.0772$ vs $0.0382$).

\textbf{The scale inversion does not transfer.} Unlike LFM2, in every arm that gives the model context (Oracle, Naive, Advanced, and Routed Modular RAG) the larger SmolLM2 model scores higher on Token F1, token precision, token recall, exact match, cosine similarity, and groundedness, significantly so (paired $p<0.05$) on all of them except exact match under Advanced RAG ($p=0.20$). Without retrieval, the 135M model has higher token recall ($0.205$ vs $0.125$). Under Naive RAG, Token F1 is $0.151$ for 360M versus $0.085$ for 135M ($\bar{d}=0.066$, 95\% CI $[0.047, 0.085]$, paired $p<0.001$, Wilcoxon $p<0.001$), the larger model wins on 252 questions against 124 (sign test $p<0.001$), and it is also higher on token recall ($0.423$ vs $0.321$), exact match ($0.038$ vs $0.020$, $p=0.03$), and the share of questions with full recall ($31.8\%$ vs $20.5\%$). The rank-based tests agree with the $t$-tests here, unlike in LFM2. In every arm, both SmolLM2 models refuse less often than either LFM2 model (between $1.0\%$ and $9.2\%$ of runs; Naive RAG $1.7\%$ for SmolLM2-135M and $1.0\%$ for SmolLM2-360M), and refusal does not explain the scale difference: on the 572 questions that neither model refuses under Naive RAG, the larger model still scores higher ($\bar{d}=0.066$, 95\% CI $[0.047, 0.086]$, paired $p<0.001$). The larger SmolLM2 model also gives shorter answers ($28.3$ vs $44.0$ generated tokens), so in both families the model with shorter answers has the higher Token F1. In LFM2 this advantage disappears at matched response length (Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion}); in SmolLM2 it does not (Figure \ref{fig:precision_recall}): within deciles of response length the larger model still scores higher ($0.040$, 95\% CI $[0.021, 0.057]$; regression at equal length $0.043$, CI $[0.025, 0.061]$; Oracle RAG $0.052$ and $0.041$). The LFM2 scale inversion therefore does not carry over to SmolLM2 and is not a general property of sub-1B models.

\textbf{The routing failure appears in one of the two SmolLM2 models.} SmolLM2-135M answered ``no'' to the routing question in 2,511 of 6,000 Routed Modular RAG runs and ``yes'' in 1,123; the remaining 2,366 replies began with neither word and therefore proceeded to retrieval, as the router specifies (Section \ref{sec:architecture}). On the 251 questions it mostly routed away from retrieval, answering without retrieval gives a Token F1 of $0.023$ against $0.084$ with Naive RAG, and retrieval helps on 107 of them and hurts on 26, the same failure as in both LFM2 models. SmolLM2-360M answered ``yes'' in 5,999 of 6,000 runs, so its Routed Modular RAG arm behaves as multi-query retrieval and scores close to Naive RAG ($0.145$ vs $0.151$ Token F1). When the SmolLM2 models did retrieve, RAG-Fusion found a gold document less often than Naive RAG: active-only Hit@3 is $0.905$ for SmolLM2-135M (349 questions) and $0.883$ for SmolLM2-360M (599 questions), against $0.945$ for Naive RAG over all 600 questions (Table \ref{tab:ir_metrics}), whereas on the 34 and 17 questions where the LFM2 models retrieved, Hit@3 is $1.000$.

\textbf{Repetitions are not identical.} Despite greedy decoding and a fixed seed, the 10 repetitions returned identical text for only 52 to 218 of the 600 questions for SmolLM2-135M and 184 to 367 for SmolLM2-360M, depending on the arm (Table \ref{tab:smollm2}); the larger model is more consistent in every arm. For SmolLM2, the repetitions therefore also vary quality, and the question-level averaging over 10 runs matters.

\section{Limitations and Scope}
\label{sec:limitations}
We explicitly note the scope boundaries of this study: (1) evaluations cover two architecture families (Liquid AI LFM2 and SmolLM2), each at two scales, and other sub-1B families remain untested; (2) experiments focus on the HotpotQA Distractor multi-hop QA benchmark, using the first 600 questions of its development split rather than a random sample, so results may not transfer to other tasks, benchmarks, or subsets; (3) Modular RAG was evaluated in one configuration, Routed Modular RAG (generator-driven routing plus RAG-Fusion), with an unconstrained prompt for the routing decision; (4) we report document-level MRR, Hit, Recall, and nDCG at rank 3 alongside a custom Answer-Term Hit Rate@3, but we do not use human relevance judgments; (5) all quality metrics are lexical, and we do not perform human or LLM-judge correctness scoring, so the relative correctness of the two model scales cannot be established from these metrics alone; (6) we evaluate no model above 700M parameters ourselves; the comparison with larger models (Section \ref{sec:larger_models}) relies on prior work with different setups; (7) the Advanced RAG ablation is at the retrieval level only, and we did not vary the number of distractor passages, so the answer-level explanations in Section \ref{sec:scale_inversion} are associations rather than causal effects; and (8) the SmolLM2 runs used a second machine and a 1,024-token generation cap, so latency is compared only within each family.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this 120,000-run empirical study, we evaluated the impact of RAG pipeline sophistication and parameter scale in two sub-1B model families, LFM2 and SmolLM2, with 60,000 runs each. The Oracle RAG gold-context reference baseline shows that 100\% gold evidence passage injection yields the highest Token F1 ($0.1384$ on LFM2-350M) and token recall, but not the highest exact match on LFM2-350M ($0.0233$ versus $0.0367$ under Naive RAG), so it serves as a gold-context reference rather than a ceiling, and generation-side extraction bottlenecks remain. Among practical retrieval-based arms, single-stage Naive RAG delivers the best observed quality-latency trade-off ($0.1107$ Token F1) at the lowest latency ($0.581$s). Multi-stage Advanced RAG takes 2.5 times the latency ($1.460$s) without statistically significant quality gain at either scale ($\bar{d}=0.0089, p=0.314$ on 350M; $p=0.539$ on 700M); a retrieval-level ablation shows that the query rewrite written by the evaluated model lowers retrieval quality, most strongly for LFM2-350M, while hybrid retrieval with reranking on the original query retrieves gold documents more often than any other configuration we tested. Routed Modular RAG (routing plus RAG-Fusion) bypasses retrieval for most queries ($0.0218$ Token F1) under our unconstrained, generator-driven prompt routing. Furthermore, within the evaluated LFM2 family, LFM2-350M has higher mean Token F1 than LFM2-700M in every retrieval-based arm (Naive RAG: $\bar{d}=0.0380, p < 0.001$; paired $t$-tests $p \le 0.017$ in the other arms), but rank-based tests are not significant in any arm and token recall is higher for LFM2-700M, so the scale effect depends on the metric. Under Naive and Oracle RAG, the F1 gap disappears or reverses when responses of similar length are compared; under Naive RAG, refusal rates do not account for it; and the raw gap is also present under Oracle RAG, where no distractor passages are present. Distractor sensitivity and its attention-level mechanism remain open hypotheses. The Routed Modular RAG router also shows that neither LFM2 model recognises when it needs retrieval. In SmolLM2, Naive RAG is again the best retrieval-based arm, but with retrieved or gold context the larger model scores higher on every quality metric, also at matched response length, so the LFM2 scale inversion does not carry over to SmolLM2; SmolLM2-135M shows the same routing failure, while SmolLM2-360M requests retrieval in almost every run. Future work should measure the answer-level effect of each Advanced RAG stage, test constrained or classifier-based routing for Routed Modular RAG, vary the number of distractor passages, score correctness with human or LLM judges, and extend the evaluation to further sub-1B families, larger models, and other tasks.
